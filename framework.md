# Framework: Call → Content

The method this skill runs on. `SKILL.md` executes these steps, this file explains the reasoning behind each one so you can judge the output quality yourself, not just trust the process blindly.

---

## The core idea

A call you've already had (a client call, a sales call, a coaching session, a workshop, a podcast-style conversation) already contains real material: specific numbers, real objections, a moment where something clicked, a line someone said that you haven't stopped thinking about. Most of that gets lost the moment the call ends.

This skill's job is to mine that raw material and turn it into finished LinkedIn posts, not a summary of the call and not a generic list of "content ideas." A content idea is information. A finished draft is an outcome.

---

## Step 1: Mining, not summarizing

Summarizing a transcript produces "we discussed X, Y, and Z." That's useless for content, nobody wants to read a meeting recap.

Mining means reading the transcript looking specifically for:

- **Emotional moments** — frustration, relief, surprise, a breakthrough
- **Concrete details** — a real number, a specific timeframe, a named tool or method
- **Quotable lines** — something said in the actual words of a real person, not paraphrased
- **Tension or contrast** — before/after, expectation vs. reality, what they thought vs. what actually happened
- **A turning point** — the moment in the call where something shifted

Each mined moment gets tagged with a likely content type:

| Content type | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| Personal story | A specific thing that happened, told as a scene |
| Contrarian take | Something said on the call that pushes against common wisdom |
| Lesson | A specific, teachable takeaway from a real situation |
| Case study | A before/after with a real (if anonymized) result |
| Behind-the-scenes | A moment that shows how the work actually happens, not the polished version |

A transcript usually produces 5-10 usable moments. Not every call has 10, some have 3, that's fine. Padding a weak transcript with generic angles produces weak posts. Better to draft 2 strong posts than 5 forgettable ones.

---

## Step 2: Choosing what to draft

Not every mined angle deserves a full draft. The skill recommends a subset (default 3) based on:

- **Diversity** — don't draft 3 posts that are all "lesson" type, mix content types
- **Specificity** — an angle with a real quote or number beats an angle that's just a topic
- **Least genericness** — if the angle could have come from any call about any topic, it's weak

You always see the full mined list before drafting starts, and you always confirm or override the recommendation. The skill never silently picks for you.

---

## Step 3: Drafting, not outlining

Every selected angle becomes one complete, ready-to-post draft, not an outline, not bullet points to expand later. The bar: could you copy this draft and post it right now, maybe after one light edit pass, and have it read like something you actually wrote?

Every draft must trace back to something specific from the transcript. If a draft could have been written without ever reading the transcript, it failed. That's the enforcement mechanism behind "outcome, not information": a real transcript detail is the receipt that proves the post is grounded in something that actually happened, not generic advice about a topic.

---

## Why this beats a generic "AI, write me a LinkedIn post" prompt

A generic prompt has nothing to work from except a topic, so it produces generic advice, the kind everyone's seen a thousand times. This skill starts from something specific that already happened in a real conversation, which is the raw material every good post actually needs: a real story, not a repackaged opinion.
